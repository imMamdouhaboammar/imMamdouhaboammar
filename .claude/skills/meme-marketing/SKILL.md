# Meme Marketing

Turn a real situation into a meme someone forwards to a friend by name, and hand the designer everything needed to build it in ten minutes.

## Why most AI-written memes die

Asked for a meme, a model reaches for a joke *about* the topic. It produces something clever, balanced and universal. Memes work the other way around. They are narrow, specific, and a little embarrassing to admit out loud.

People share a meme to say **"this is me"** or **"this is you"**. Recognition is the engine, not cleverness. A joke that could belong to anyone gets a smile and dies. A moment that belongs to one person on one specific Thursday afternoon gets sent to three people by name.

So this work is closer to **remembering** than to writing. You are hunting for a moment that already happened to the audience, not inventing a situation that plausibly could.

The second thing that kills AI memes is polish. Real memes are typed fast on a phone. They carry the fingerprints of that: no final period, a stretched letter, a word in the wrong script, a small typo nobody bothered to fix. Clean, balanced, grammatically complete copy reads as an ad, and ads do not get forwarded.

## The two mouths

Every meme here has two voices, and keeping them separate is the whole trick.

- **The caption is the narrator.** It sets the situation from outside, and it usually ends with a colon. The colon is a door: it tells the reader to stop and look down.
- **The line inside the image is the character.** It is spoken by whoever is in the picture, in their voice and their vocabulary, not the narrator's.

When both lines come out of the same mouth, the meme collapses into a sentence with a picture stapled to it. If the line inside the image is summarizing, restating, or explaining the caption, you have one mouth. Rewrite.

Test it by reading them aloud back to back. Two different people should be talking.

## The mechanism, not the shape

Copying the shape of a meme produces a meme-shaped ad. The mechanism is:

1. The caption makes the reader predict what comes next.
2. The image confirms the *feeling* they predicted.
3. The line inside the image lands somewhere slightly to the left of what they expected.

The gap between 2 and 3 is where the laugh lives. If the line delivers exactly what the caption promised, there is no gap and no laugh, just agreement.

One important consequence for tone: **the contrast belongs in the structure, never in the sentence.** The caption and the image already carry the opposition. A punchline that spells the opposition out in words (denial then reveal, "not this, this", "ده مش كذا ده كذا") is doing the picture's job badly and reads instantly as written-by-a-machine. Let the juxtaposition stay visual.

## The engine

Work through these in order. Steps 1 to 3 are the whole game; most bad memes are bad because someone skipped straight to 6.

### 1. Lock the audience to one person

"Marketers" is not an audience. "A junior designer, two years in, at a mid-size Cairo agency, who gets revisions at 11pm from an account manager who never opens the file" is an audience.

Write that person down in one sentence before anything else. If the brief does not give you enough to do this, ask for it, or name the assumption out loud and proceed.

Their job title, their seniority, the size of their company, the city, the tools they open every day, who they report to, what they complain about in the group chat. All of that becomes material.

### 2. Mine moments, not jokes

Generate **12 to 20 micro-moments** from that person's week. Not punchlines. Moments. The bar is: could this have happened yesterday, to a real person, exactly this way.

Good moments share three traits:

- **Repetitive.** It happens often enough that they have a feeling about it.
- **Small.** The tighter the frame, the harder the recognition. Not "clients are difficult", but "the client replies to the quote with a voice note at 1am".
- **Slightly humiliating, or quietly absurd.** The stuff people joke about to avoid being annoyed about it.

Pull receipts while you mine. Real numbers (a salary figure, a deadline, a count of revisions), real phrases people actually say, real times of day, real tool names. The receipt is what proves you were there.

Do not evaluate yet. Quantity first, and let the weird ones live.

### 3. Pick by the "دي أنا" test

For each candidate ask: **would a specific person tag a specific other person under this?**

If the answer is "people would find it relatable", that is a no. Relatable-in-general is the sound of a dead meme. You want "Ahmed will send this to Mostafa within an hour, and Mostafa will reply with a voice note".

Keep the three or four that survive. Kill the rest without ceremony.

### 4. Name the emotional state in one word

This is the hinge between the writing and the visual. Force yourself to one word: استسلام، إنكار، صدمة، إحراج، شماتة، نشوة، رعب هادي، فخر مقلوب، تبلد.

The word is what you take shopping when you go looking for a face. A precise emotional word gets you a precise visual. A vague one ("محبط") gets you a stock photo.

### 5. Match the visual

Read `references/format-bank.md` and pick a format whose face already carries that exact state, and that this specific audience has seen before.

Two directions work, and both are in the bank:

- **Localize a global format.** Take a template the audience knows from the internet and drag it into their world (the Jerry-in-bed meme, but Jerry is wearing a hijab and crying, in an Egyptian bedroom).
- **Update a local reference.** Take a film line or a scene the audience has quoted since they were kids, and swap one word so it lands inside their job.

Do not invent a visual nobody has seen. Recognition is half the joke, and you only get it from shared memory.

### 6. Write the caption

Read `references/caption-craft.md` for the seven setup patterns and the language rules. Pick the pattern that fits the moment; do not default to "لما" every time just because it is the most common.

### 7. Write the line inside the image

This is the craft move most worth getting right. Read the caption-craft file's section on the one-word swap.

Three ways, in order of how well they usually land:

1. **Keep the original line.** If the scene's own famous line already fits, use it untouched and let the audience do the work.
2. **Swap one word.** Take the line everyone has memorized and change a single word so it enters the audience's world. Keeping the original rhythm is what makes it funny; rewriting the whole line breaks the reference and the laugh with it.
3. **Write a fresh line in that character's voice.** Only when the scene has no quotable line. Match the character's register and length exactly.

Length rule: **the line inside the image is always shorter than the caption.** One line about 70% of the time, two when the joke genuinely needs a condition. Three lines is almost always a sign the idea is not sharp yet.

### 8. Spec the design

Read `references/design-spec.md` and fill in the design block. Do not leave it as "make it look like a meme". The designer should not have to make a single decision you could have made for them.

### 9. Run the six gates

Before you show anything, put each meme through the gates below. Memes fail these quietly, and a failed meme costs more than a missing one because it makes the brand look like it is trying.

## The six gates

**1. The "دي أنا" gate.** Can you name the person who sends it and the person who receives it? If not, the moment is too broad.

**2. The two mouths gate.** Read the caption, then the line in the image. Two different people talking? If the punchline is restating the caption, rewrite it.

**3. The receipt gate.** Is there one specific detail that proves lived experience? A number, a phrase, a time, a tool name. If every noun in the meme is generic, add the receipt.

**4. The remove-the-brand gate.** Cover the logo. Would anyone still share this? If the joke only works because you know it is an ad, it is an ad.

**5. The compression gate.** Delete every word that is not carrying weight, then read it again. Comedy is timing and timing is length. Most first drafts lose 30% of their words and get funnier.

**6. The explanation gate.** If the meme needs a beat of thinking, or if you feel the urge to add a clarifying line, it is dead. Memes are understood at scroll speed or not at all. Wordplay and puns almost always fail this gate, because they ask the reader to work.

## Output format

Deliver each meme in this block. Keep the labels; they are what makes the spec usable by a designer who was not in the conversation.

```
### ميم [N] — [اسم الموقف في تلات كلمات]

الجمهور: [الشخص الواحد المحدد]
الحالة: [كلمة واحدة]

الكابشن:
[النص زي ما هينشر بالظبط]

النص جوه الصورة:
[سطر واحد، أقصر من الكابشن]

الفيجوال:
الفورمات: [اسم الفورمات أو وصف المشهد بدقة كفاية إن الديزاينر يلاقيه]
الوصف: [إيه اللي في الكادر وإيه الحالة على الوش]
بديل: [فورمات تاني بنفس الحالة لو الأول مش متاح]

التصميم:
المقاس: [1:1 / 4:5 / 16:9] | النص: [مكانه وعدد سطوره] | اللوجو: [الركن]

ليه هيشتغل: [سطر واحد عن آلية التعرف، مش شرح للنكتة]
```

When the user asks for a batch, also give them **one caption with three different visuals** for at least one idea. That is how you test which face carries the moment best without rewriting the copy, and it is standard practice in high-output meme accounts.

Default to **3 memes** when the number is not specified. Offer more rather than dumping twenty half-formed ones.

## Putting the brand in without killing the meme

The brand enters as **a character in the story**, never as a sponsor of it.

Working: the course is the thing the character is secretly attending, the product is what the rival team has, the tool is the reason the character is suddenly calm. The audience learns the product exists through the plot.

Not working: the punchline names a benefit, the caption ends with a call to action, the meme explains why the product solves the problem in the image.

Keep product-forward memes to roughly **one in five**. The other four buy the right to post the fifth.

**No CTA, no link, no hashtags, no price, no feature list inside the meme.** The small logo in the corner is the entire branding budget of the format. Anything more and the share rate drops, because sharing an obvious ad costs the sharer social credit.

If the user needs a conversion push, put it in the first comment or the following post, and say so in your delivery.

## Dialect

Egyptian is the default. Switch to Saudi/Gulf when the brief names a Gulf audience, and then switch the cultural references too, not just the vocabulary. A Gulf meme with Egyptian film references reads as imported and gets no recognition. `references/format-bank.md` has a Gulf section for this.

English is available and uses the same engine, with global formats and English-language receipts.

Never mix dialects inside a single meme, and never write a meme in Modern Standard Arabic. Fully correct MSA in a meme signals a press release and kills the share instantly.
